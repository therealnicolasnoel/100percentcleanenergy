## Where we are now

In 2024, low-carbon sources (renewables and nuclear) generated about 41% of the world's electricity, according to Ember's Global Electricity Review 2025. Fossil fuels supplied the remaining ~59%. Solar was the largest source of new generation for the third year running.

The metric is the share of total generation, measured in terawatt-hours, that comes from sources with near-zero operating emissions. It does not count captured fossil generation unless capture is near-complete.

## What has to be true

1. Clean generation grows faster than demand, which is rising with electrification of transport, heat, industry and data centres.
2. Grids can carry and balance variable output: transmission, interconnection and flexibility are built at the same pace as generation.
3. Enough firm clean power exists for long low-wind, low-sun periods: storage, hydro, nuclear, geothermal or other always-on sources.
4. Capital reaches emerging and developing economies at a cost of finance that makes clean projects cheaper than fossil ones.
5. Existing fossil plants are retired or repurposed rather than kept running alongside new clean capacity.

## Main bottlenecks

- **Grids (high).** Connection queues and slow permitting for transmission now delay more projects than the cost of panels or turbines.
- **Cost of capital (high).** In many emerging economies, financing costs are several times higher than in advanced ones, which erodes the cost advantage of capital-heavy clean power.
- **Firm power and long-duration storage (medium).** Batteries cover hours; seasonal and multi-day gaps still rely mostly on fossil plants. See problems 12 and 20.
- **Locked-in fossil fleets (medium).** Young coal plants in Asia have decades of expected life and contracts that make early closure costly.

## Paths

- **Solar and wind with storage and much larger grids.** The cheapest path per unit of energy; most dependent on transmission and flexibility.
- **A mix with firm clean power.** Adds nuclear, geothermal and hydro to reduce how much storage and overbuild is needed; slower and costlier to build.
- **Demand-side flexibility.** Shifting when electric vehicles, heat pumps and industry draw power reduces the need for both of the above.

## What would change this assessment

- Faster: transmission permitting reform in major markets, or long-duration storage falling below $20 per kWh (problem 12).
- Slower: interest rates staying high for emerging economies, or electricity demand growing faster than clean supply.

## What you can do

- **Researchers:** improve open data on grid connection queues and curtailment by country.
- **Builders:** work on grid-enhancing technologies and faster interconnection studies.
- **Funders:** back blended-finance vehicles that lower the cost of capital for clean projects in emerging economies.
- **Policymakers:** set binding timelines for transmission permits.
